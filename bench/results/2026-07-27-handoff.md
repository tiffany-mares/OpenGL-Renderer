# Input-handoff benchmark — 2026-07-27

## Provenance

- **Date:** 2026-07-27 (run 23:39–23:51 EDT; raw dir `bench/results/raw/20260727-233953/`, git-ignored)
- **Binary:** built from commit `008b59a`, Release, MSVC (Visual Studio 16 2019 generator, MSVC 19.29)
- **Machine:** Intel(R) Core(TM) Ultra 7 155H; Intel(R) Arc(TM) Graphics, GL_VERSION `3.3.0 - Build 32.0.101.8132`; Windows 11 Home; AC power; machine otherwise idle for the full run
- **Protocol:** three parts. (1) Uncontended per-op cost: `handoff_bench`, 1,000,000 iterations × 5 reps per backend/op, batched median. (2) In-app cells: `cube --input=<backend> --fps=144 --pace=timer_spin --bench-frames=10000 --poll-hz={1000,10000}` — 6 cells (mutex/bitmask/seqlock × 1 kHz/10 kHz poll), 10,000 frames each, first 500 discarded as warmup (n=9500); the render thread consumes once per frame at 144 Hz; `input_latency_ns` = consume time − payload `publish_ns`, same monotonic clock. (3) Contended sweep: `handoff_bench` pins a tight-loop reader against a paced publisher at 1 k/10 k/100 k/1 M/unthrottled publishes/s, 2 s windows, read latency sampled every 32nd read. Each timed sweep sample carries one clock pair (~47 ns), identical across backends, so it cancels in cross-backend comparison. No thread pinning anywhere.
- **Methodology note — batched means are amortized throughput.** Table A/B cost numbers come from 1 M-iteration timed batches: the loop overlaps successive iterations (ILP, branch prediction, hot caches), so they are amortized per-op throughput figures, not single-call latency. They are comparable to each other; they are not "what one isolated call costs."

## Machine notes

- **Two-spinner contention: judge app latency against the ACHIEVED poll rate.** Each app
  cell runs two concurrent `timer_spin` busy-wait pacers — the render pacer (144 Hz) and
  the poll pacer (`--poll-hz`) — competing for cores. At a 1 kHz target the poll loop
  achieved 967.9–970.7 Hz with 2.7–3.8% missed deadlines; at 10 kHz it achieved only
  3068–3364 Hz with ~46–51% missed (reported as-measured below, not hidden). All latency
  percentiles in Table B are therefore interpreted against each cell's achieved period
  (from its `handoff:` line), not the nominal target. (An earlier on-battery run was worse
  still — 805–981 Hz at the 1 kHz target — AC power helps but does not eliminate the
  spinner-vs-spinner contention.)
- **1 kHz p99 latency sits in missed-poll gaps, not backend cost.** With >1% of publishes
  late, the 99th-percentile sample necessarily lands in a missed-deadline gap: p99 is
  2.75–3.36 ms against a ~1.03 ms achieved period. p50 behaves: seqlock 529 µs ≈ half the
  1033 µs achieved period (516 µs expected); mutex 639 µs is ~24% above half-period,
  consistent with its slightly higher miss count (2570 vs 1813) dragging the distribution.
  Critically, p99 is nowhere near the 15.6 ms scheduler tick — the paced poll loop did
  not regress to tick-granularity sleeping.
- **10 kHz cells: length-biased sampling of an irregular publisher.** At ~50% missed
  deadlines the publish gaps are highly irregular, and a reader sampling at a random
  point in time is length-biased toward long gaps (inspection paradox): p50 lands near
  the full ~325 µs achieved mean period rather than half of it. These cells measure the
  machine's inability to hold 10 kHz under two spinners, not the backends.
- **The mutex-vs-seqlock p99 differences in Table B are scheduling noise, not backend
  properties.** At 10 kHz the seqlock cell shows the *higher* p99 (5.1 ms vs 2.0 ms) —
  the reverse of the per-op cost ordering — and that same cell shows elevated CPU
  (38.1% vs 21.1%), a longer wall time, and 1081 missed render deadlines (vs ≤14 for the
  mutex cells): it simply drew heavier scheduler contention. The controlled sweep
  (Table C) is where backend differences are real; there the ordering is consistent.
- **The one nonzero in-app retry count is a single writer-preemption event, and it is
  the seqlock hazard on display.** `read_retries()` counts retry-loop *iterations*.
  94,634 iterations across a 74.7 s run (~10,500 reads) at ~10 ns/iteration is ~1 ms of
  total spinning — one (or a very few) events where the OS preempted the publisher
  mid-write (sequence odd) and the reader spun until the writer was rescheduled. That is
  the textbook seqlock failure mode under core oversubscription, produced here only in
  the overloaded 10 kHz cell.

## Crossover

From Table C: the mutex reader's p99 stays within one 100 ns clock quantum of the
seqlock's through 10,000 publishes/s (200 ns vs 100 ns — at the measurement floor). The
first measurable departure is at **≈100,000 publishes/s** (300 ns vs 100 ns), and by
1,000,000 publishes/s the divergence is unambiguous (1,300 ns vs 200 ns, 6.5×; 2,600 ns
vs 600 ns unthrottled). The measured crossover — the rate below which choosing the
seqlock over the mutex buys no observable read-tail improvement — is therefore about
**10⁵ publishes/s: two orders of magnitude above this app's real 1 kHz publish rate.**
